The chain

Select a stage. Each one is a pure transformation: something goes in, something else comes out, and your build recorded a number for it.

Three words that carry everything

Term
A string in the dictionary — the unit the index is keyed by. Not a word: a term is whatever survived your preprocessing. In the stemmed index, technology and technologies are both the single term technologi.
Posting
One entry saying "this term occurs in this document". A posting is just a document ID. In InvertedIndex the postings for a term are held as a sorted list of ints — sorted, because every merge algorithm downstream depends on it.
Document frequency (df)
How many documents a term appears in, which is exactly the length of its postings list. Not the same as collection frequency (cf), which counts every occurrence. Both live in Vocabulary; df drives retrieval, cf drives the frequency table below.
Across the stemmed index the mean df is 13.383 and 9,505 terms occur exactly once (hapax legomena). Most of the dictionary is nearly useless for retrieval; a handful of terms do almost all the work.

The three operators share one loop and differ only in what they do with each outcome. AND emits only on equality. OR emits on every branch. AND NOT emits when A is strictly smaller — that is, when a document is in A and cannot be in B. Note that AND NOT never builds the complement of B, which would be a list of thousands of documents.

Porter stemmer, step by step

Stemming is what collapses 33,805 terms to 25,049. It is not a dictionary lookup — it is five ordered groups of suffix rules, each firing at most once. The first rule in a group whose pattern matches, and whose measure condition holds, wins. Pick a word to see the trace your implementation recorded.

Measure m is the count of vowel–consonant alternations in the stem. It is the algorithm's only guard against over-stemming: a rule with condition m > 1 refuses to strip a suffix off a short stem. It is also the source of the failures in section 5.

Why ordering AND arguments by df pays

An intersection can never be larger than its smallest input. So if you merge the two shortest lists first, every subsequent merge starts from a small accumulator. Merge the longest first and you carry a large intermediate result through the whole chain. The result set is identical either way — only the work changes.

Wildcards as a set intersection

A wildcard pattern cannot be looked up in the dictionary, so KGramIndex converts it into terms that can. The pattern is padded with $ sentinels, split on *, and each literal run of length ≥ 3 yields 3-grams. Intersecting those k-gram postings gives candidates. Then — and this stage is not optional — a regex removes the candidates that contain the right pieces in the wrong order.

Edit distance as a grid

Levenshtein distance is a dynamic program. Cell (i, j) is the cheapest way to turn the first i characters of one string into the first j of the other, and it depends only on its three neighbours. Select any cell to see the three candidates it minimised over.

Select a cell in the grid.

The highlighted path is one optimal alignment, traced back from the bottom-right corner. levenshtein() in tolerant.py keeps only two rows, so it computes this number without ever building the whole grid; levenshtein_matrix() builds it in full for exactly this kind of display.

The cap argument matters more than it looks. If every value in a row already exceeds the cap, no later row can come back under it, so the function abandons early. Without it you would pay full DP cost against tens of thousands of hopeless candidates.

Five configurations, one trade-off

Each preprocessing step trades precision for recall. The five configurations are cumulative, and the plot shows where each lands. Nothing here is free.

Read the shape, not just the winner. Lowercasing is nearly free: it takes macro precision from 0.866 to 1.000 and recall from 0.644 to 0.869, because case variation was pure noise. Stop-word removal changes the scores by nothing at all — identical to normalised on all six figures — because no evaluation query rests on a stop word. Stemming is the only step that genuinely trades: precision falls from 1.000 to 0.906 while recall climbs to 0.956. Lemmatising is the odd one out: it improves precision over stemming but its macro F1, 0.865, is the second worst of the five.

Vocabulary collapse

Each cumulative step shrinks the dictionary. The size of the drop tells you how much variation that step was treating as noise.

Lowercasing removes 4,285 types — pure case variants. Stop-word removal removes only 176 more types, but 101,114 postings and 357,654 tokens: stop words are few but everywhere. Stemming removes 8,756 types, by far the largest structural collapse, and drops hapax legomena from 12,697 to 9,505. Lemmatising is far more conservative than stemming: 30,821 types against 25,049, which is why it recovers precision and loses recall.

Tolerant retrieval: a large gain, honestly priced

Correction repairs 18 of the 20 misspellings and recall rises more than sixfold. The cost is visible in micro precision, which falls from 0.984 to 0.919. Exact matching almost never returned a wrong document because it usually returned nothing at all; correction returns documents, and about one retrieved document in twelve is now wrong. Macro-averaging softens this, because it weights a query returning three documents the same as one returning three hundred.

Repair runs against the unstemmed vocabulary and the result is stemmed afterwards. Correcting against stemmed terms fails outright: goverment stems to gover, which exists, so a stem-based corrector sees a valid term and never reaches government.

Known failures

Four findings from your own run. None of these are bugs in the implementation; all four are consequences of the algorithms behaving exactly as specified.

1. The stemmer splits the technology family

technology → technologi technologies → technologi technological → technolog

Step 1a rewrites ies → i, which puts technology and technologies together on technologi. But technological reaches step 2, where ical → ic and related rules apply, and lands on technolog. Porter's 1980 algorithm has no rule bridging technologi and technolog, because it works on suffixes in isolation and never consults a dictionary of related forms.

The consequence is visible in your index: technologi has df 241 while technolog is a separate posting list entirely. Query q26, technology, retrieves only the first, so every document that says only "technological" is lost. That is recall gone for no linguistic reason.

2. player never reaches play

player → player players → player play → plai playing → plai played → plai

Step 4 has a rule stripping -er, but it is conditioned on m(stem) > 1. The stem here is play, whose measure is exactly 1, so the rule declines to fire and player stays intact. Meanwhile play itself is caught by step 1c, which rewrites a terminal y to i when the stem contains a vowel, giving plai.

So the family splits three ways rather than two, and the two halves diverge in opposite directions: player keeps its suffix while play gains a mutation. Query q27 players resolves to player, df 376, and never sees any document that talks only about playing. The measure guard is doing its job — it exists to stop over-stemming short words — and the cost is this.

3. Lowercasing merges the country US into the pronoun us

Normalisation lowercases unconditionally, so US and us become one term. The obvious fix would be stop-word removal, but the NLTK English stop-word list does not contain us — it has we, our and ours, but not the objective form. So the term survives every stage of the pipeline.

The result is the second row of the frequency table: us with 3,478 occurrences, sitting between said and mr. That single postings list conflates every American business story with every sentence containing "told us" or "for us". A query for the country cannot separate them, and neither can a query trying to avoid it.

This is the classic argument for case-sensitive handling of acronyms, or for a collection-specific stop list rather than an off-the-shelf one.

4. Edit distance is frequency-blind, so 2 of 20 repairs go to the wrong word

d(labuor, labor) = 1 d(labuor, labour) = 2 d(pirce, piece) = 1 d(pirce, price) = 2

Plain Levenshtein charges a transposition as two substitutions, so the intended repair sits two edits away while an unrelated word sits one edit away and wins. Query m16 asks for labuor and receives the American spelling labor rather than the British labour the BBC corpus actually uses. Query m09 asks for pirce and receives piece rather than price, turning a finance query into a general-vocabulary one and scoring zero.

Distance alone cannot rank corrections, because it measures only the shape of the word and knows nothing about how likely each candidate is. A working corrector needs a channel model — how probable is this particular typo — multiplied by a prior over words. Damerau–Levenshtein, which charges a transposition as a single edit, would fix both of these cases specifically; the frequency-blindness would remain.

The frequency floor in nearest() is a partial mitigation only. It stops a hapax term winning a tie, but labor and piece are both common enough to clear it.
